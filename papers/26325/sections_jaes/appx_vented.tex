\section{Why sealed, not vented}
\label{app:vented}

A vented (ported) box promises to extend flat output roughly an octave
lower than a sealed box of the same volume, with one driver instead of two.
Repackaging the box stiffness as an acoustic compliance $C_{ab}=\Vb/(\rho_0c^2)$
and adding a port of acoustic mass/resistance $M_{ap},R_{ap}$ driven by box
pressure $P_b$ gives the classical 4th-order vented alignment
\cite{small1973vented} in \eqref{eq:ode}'s own notation, so every sealed-box
quantity ($\sigma$, $\ws$, $\Cms$) transfers unchanged. As
$M_{ap},R_{ap}\to\infty$ this specializes exactly to the sealed operator
\eqref{eq:ode} (a shut port is a sealed box) --- the vented model adds
physics rather than replacing it.

\begin{proposition}[$24$\,dB/octave, not $12$]
\label{prop:vented24}
For an ideal port, low-frequency output falls as $\omega^4$: vented slope is
$24$\,dB/oct, vs.\ sealed's $12$. A real port ($R_{ap}>0$) crosses from
$18$ to $24$\,dB/oct well below tuning for any reasonably low-loss port, so
this steeper roll-off governs essentially the whole sub-tuning band of
interest.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Excursion is not box-limited]
\label{prop:ventedxmax}
Below tuning, cone excursion relaxes to the driver's own free-air DC limit,
independent of box or port sizing: the box supplies \emph{no} restoring
force below $F_b$, unlike sealed, where $\wc^2$ bounds $x$ at every
frequency down to DC. At fixed drive and box volume, vented moves
$(1+\alpha)=(F_c/F_s)^2$ times farther than the same driver sealed in the
same box --- more than double for the worked example's $\Qtc=0.71$ box.
\end{proposition}

Standard practice counters this with a steep high-pass filter just below
$F_b$; but that filter necessarily removes the same sub-tuning output
whose room-gain rescue (\cref{sec:roomclose}) is sealed's chief
low-frequency advantage, so the fix and the case for choosing vented pull
in opposite directions.

The sealed room-gain match of \cref{sec:roomclose} ($F_c\simeq
f_{\mathrm{pz}}$, $-12$\,dB/oct meeting the room's flat pressure-zone term)
has no vented analogue: $-24$\,dB/oct is twice as steep, and closing the
gap with FIR would drive an already excursion-unbounded cone harder, with
no $\Xmax$-derived ceiling to say when to stop.

Near tuning the cone nulls and essentially all output passes through the
port; port air velocity at the target is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\ v_{\mathrm{port}}\approx\frac{2\pi r\,p_t}{\rho_0\,\omega_b\,
S_{\mathrm{port}}}\ }.
\label{eq:vport}
\end{equation}
Turbulence (``chuffing'') onset is empirically $v_{\max}\approx17$\,m/s
\cite{dickason2006}. For the running $60$\,m$^3$/$110$\,dB/$F_b=23$\,Hz
example:

A single $10$\,cm round port already runs at $\sim5\times$ the
turbulence-onset velocity; holding $v\le v_{\max}$ needs a
$\approx23$\,cm port --- a real, level-dependent, compressive,
un-equalizable distortion and noise mechanism with no sealed-box analogue.

Three independent, first-principles reasons therefore support the sealed
choice: a steeper low-frequency asymptote (\cref{prop:vented24}), an
excursion no longer bounded by the box at all below tuning
(\cref{prop:ventedxmax}), and a room-gain mechanism (\cref{sec:roomclose})
that has no vented equivalent, on top of the port-velocity liability above.
These hold within this paper's DSP-corrected, room-integrated premise
(A6); where amplifier headroom, not distortion or room gain, is the
binding constraint, a vented alignment's higher passband sensitivity
remains a legitimate, narrower-purpose choice.
